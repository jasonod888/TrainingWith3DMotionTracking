# Refined Product Concept: AI Interactive Movement Learning SaaS

I think you've arrived at a stronger product concept. Rather than choosing between a personal-learning application and a commercial training platform, we can combine both into one SaaS product, using the same underlying technology and user experience.

The key is to treat every uploaded video as a reusable interactive lesson, with the person uploading it deciding whether to use it privately, share it for free, or sell access.

That means you're building neither a conventional course marketplace nor just a pose-tracking application.

You're building a video-to-interactive-learning platform for human movement.

## 1. The core product proposition

PROPOSED POSITIONING

# Turn any movement video into an interactive lesson.

Learn movements yourself, or create interactive training experiences that others can follow, practise and master.

Learn for yourself

Upload a demonstration, generate a 3D reference and practise privately.

Teach others

Create lessons and share them with students, clients or paying customers.

One common movement-learning engine

SAM 3D Body + MediaPipe + interactive 3D player

The important point is that a personal learner and a commercial creator should not need separate products or even separate accounts.

Someone might initially register to learn a Taekwondo pattern, discover the value of the platform, and later decide to publish their own tutorials.

That progression should feel completely natural.

## 2. One account, three ways to use a lesson

I would design the system around three primary lesson types.

Private lesson — Learn yourself

The user uploads a video and receives an interactive lesson that only they can access.

For example, someone uploads a Taekwondo poomsae video and uses the 3D avatar to practise at home.

No publishing, clients or payments are involved.

Free shared lesson — Teach others

A creator uploads a demonstration and generates an interactive lesson. They can distribute access freely to students or clients.

For example, a dance instructor shares a lesson with 30 students to practise between classes.

Access can be controlled through a link, invitation or student account.

Paid lesson — Sell training content

A creator uploads their instructional video, converts it into an interactive lesson, sets a price and distributes the purchase link.

For example, a martial arts instructor sells access to a specialised kicking technique tutorial.

The platform manages payment, access permissions and the learner experience.

This is the fundamental design principle I would adopt:

Every lesson starts private. Its owner can later publish it for free or make it paid, provided they have the necessary content rights.

We should separate who can discover a lesson from who can access it. A creator may want a paid lesson that is accessible only through a private sales link, without being listed publicly.

## 3. How the actual SaaS would work

User creates an account

Uploads a demonstration video

SAM 3D Body creates reference motion

3D rig, temporal animation and movement data

Interactive lesson generated

3D avatar, video playback and camera-based matching

Keep private

Share free

Sell access

I would keep this workflow extremely simple. The user shouldn't have to learn skeletal animation, pose modelling or 3D software.

They should only need to upload a video, preview its converted movement, and decide what to do with the lesson.

## 4. Define the difference between a lesson and a course

This is an important product design decision.

I would define a lesson as one converted video, and a course as a collection of lessons.

For example:

Course: Taekwondo White Belt Fundamentals

01

Ready stance

One interactive lesson

02

Low block

One interactive lesson

03

Front kick

One interactive lesson

04

Taegeuk Il Jang

One interactive lesson

This distinction lets you gradually introduce course bundles, subscriptions and learning pathways without complicating the initial system.

For the MVP, one video = one interactive lesson is enough. Users can sell or distribute individual lessons. Course bundles can come later.

## 6. What should the creator's dashboard contain?

The creator dashboard should be an extension of the personal library—not a completely separate application.

| Section       | Function                                                 |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| My Lessons    | All converted videos, private and published              |
| Create Lesson | Upload, convert, preview and save                        |
| Publish       | Select private, free or paid access                      |
| My Students   | People granted access to published lessons               |
| Sales         | Purchases, earnings and refunds                          |
| Analytics     | Views, practice sessions and aggregate movement progress |

The most important feature is one-click publishing after the 3D animation has been reviewed.

For paid content, creators should be able to set their own prices and let your platform manage access and checkout.

You could use Stripe Connect to onboard eligible creators, route payments, collect platform fees and support payouts. ## 7. How should your SaaS make money?

I'd separate conversion charges from commercial distribution charges.

Why? Because SAM 3D Body GPU processing has a cost, but once a lesson is generated, the same reference motion can be reused by many students. Live MediaPipe processing can happen locally in the browser.

Here is a preliminary pricing structure—not yet validated against actual GPU costs or customer demand.

| Plan        | Intended user                    | Monetization                                   |
| ----------- | -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Free        | First-time learners              | Limited short private-video conversions        |
| Personal    | Individuals practising movements | Monthly subscription or conversion credits     |
| Creator     | Instructors sharing lessons      | Monthly subscription with usage allowances     |
| Creator Pro | Instructors selling lessons      | Higher subscription and/or percentage of sales |
| Academy     | Schools with many learners       | Volume-based subscription                      |

I'd consider a hybrid model in which users receive monthly processing credits and pay for additional conversions. That protects you against unusually long or repeated uploads.

Free published lessons also have operating costs. Creators might receive a fixed allowance of active students or practice usage, with higher limits on paid plans.

One principle I'd retain: don't charge a learner every time they repeat a movement if pose tracking is happening locally and the session has minimal server cost. Repetition is the behaviour your platform should encourage.

## 9. What I would launch first

I would deliberately avoid building a public marketplace or searchable course catalogue at launch.

The platform should host and sell lessons, but creators should bring their own students initially.

A Taekwondo instructor could publish a lesson and share a private purchase link through WhatsApp, email, their website or social media. This avoids the expense of building a two-sided marketplace and lets you validate whether students are actually willing to pay.

Recommended MVP scope

User registration and personal lesson library

Video upload and SAM 3D Body motion conversion

3D lesson preview and basic trim/quality controls

Student camera tracking using MediaPipe

Overlay and side-by-side learning modes

Visual movement comparison and replay controls

Private or free published lessons with access controls

Paid lessons with checkout and creator payouts

Basic creator dashboard for learners and sales

Before implementing the paid-publishing workflow, I would also add explicit content-rights checks. A user might be permitted to practise privately with a video but not entitled to resell a lesson derived from it. Commercial publication should require appropriate rights to the footage, music and other protected content. Keeping lessons private does not automatically resolve copyright restrictions either.

The platform should also protect student camera privacy, minimise retention of recorded sessions, and have suitable safeguards if children use it.

## 10. The product architecture should reflect this model

User Account / Lesson Library

Video → 3D Processing

SAM 3D Body + temporal animation reconstruction

Reusable Interactive Lesson

3D asset + movement data + student-facing player

Learning Engine

MediaPipe, comparison, playback and practice

Publishing Engine

Permissions, payments, enrollments and reporting

Notice that the publishing engine is independent of the learning engine.

This is strategically important. It means we can eventually add white-label academies, course bundles, embeddable players or even licensing to other platforms without redesigning the actual movement-processing system.

## 11. My suggested final positioning

I would position the product around two promises.

FOR INDIVIDUALS

# See it. Learn it. Do it.

Turn a demonstration into a personal interactive lesson and practise the movements using your own camera.

FOR CREATORS AND INSTRUCTORS

# Don't just show your students. Let them practise.

Transform your instructional videos into interactive lessons that students can access for free or purchase.

Both promises come from the same product.

### What I would prioritise commercially

I'd start with an open registration model where anyone can create private lessons, and creators can upgrade to publish and monetize.

The real business opportunity is that you don't need to choose whether someone is a learner or a creator when they sign up.

The same person can be both.

I would also preserve one crucial principle: this is a movement-learning platform first and a course-selling platform second. If the visual practice experience is substantially better than following an ordinary video, the paid content and business use cases become much easier to justify.

Your successful SAM 3D Body test establishes a promising reconstruction foundation. The remaining technical milestone is proving that those animations work reliably in a complete student-facing experience, with MediaPipe tracking and fair movement comparison.

Once that's demonstrated, I think this unified personal-plus-commercial SaaS model is a strong direction to validate.
